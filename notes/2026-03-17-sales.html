The Jobs to Be Done Framework: Why Your Product Features Are Almost Irrelevant


Most sales conversations fail not because your product is weak, but because you're solving the wrong problem. This is the central insight of the Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) framework, originally developed by Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen and refined by practitioners like Bob Moesta and Intercom's Des Traynor. The framework inverts how we typically think about selling—instead of leading with features and benefits, you start by understanding what job the customer is genuinely trying to accomplish in their life or business.


Here's the critical distinction: a customer doesn't buy a quarter-inch drill. They buy a quarter-inch hole. But even that's surface-level thinking. Dig deeper, and they actually buy the ability to hang shelves, which gives them a sense of accomplishment and self-reliance. That emotional and functional layering is where JTBD lives. When you map this properly, you realize your competitor isn't another CRM platform—it's the customer's current chaotic spreadsheet system, their email chaos, or even their decision to do nothing because the pain hasn't become acute enough yet.


Christensen famously illustrated this with the milkshake study. A fast-food chain was puzzled why their milkshakes weren't selling better despite product improvements. Researchers discovered something unexpected: customers weren't buying milkshakes for taste or nutrition. They were buying them for a specific job—something filling to consume during a boring morning commute. Once they understood that job, everything changed about how they marketed and positioned the product.


In B2B sales, this framework manifests in deeper discovery conversations. Rather than pitching features, you're interviewing customers about the circumstances surrounding their problem. What does their day look like right now? What's frustrating them? What have they tried before, and why didn't it work? What would "solving this" actually enable them to do? The jobs framework recognizes there are functional jobs (tasks to complete), emotional jobs (how someone wants to feel), and social jobs (what someone wants others to think of them). A CFO buying financial planning software needs it to work well, yes, but they also need to feel confident in board meetings and want to be seen as a strategic leader, not just an executor of spreadsheets.


What makes JTBD so powerful for sellers is that it creates genuine competitive advantage. When you understand the job better than your competitors do, you can position your solution not as "the best" but as "the one that actually solves what matters." You'll notice that products with seemingly superior features often lose to competitors that simply understood the customer's job more deeply. Slack didn't win because it was technically superior to existing communication tools—it won because its creators understood the job was "frictionless team communication that doesn't require formal discipline," not just "a place to send messages."


The framework also protects you from what Christensen calls the "innovation trap"—blindly improving your product features while customers silently churn because you're still solving the wrong job. Top B2B salespeople using JTBD spend proportionally less time talking and more time listening. They ask about context, about emotions, about failed attempts. They probe for the gap between the current state and the desired state, and most importantly, they understand why the customer hasn't already solved this themselves.


💡 One thing to try today: In your next sales call, after someone describes their problem, ask: "Walk me through what happens next if nothing changes. What becomes harder for you or your team?" Then listen for the emotional and social dimensions of their job, not just the functional task.
