The Sandler Submarine: Reframing Objections as Information You Actually Need


Most salespeople treat objections like obstacles—unwelcome speed bumps between them and the signature line. David Sandler, who built a billion-dollar training empire starting in Baltimore in the 1960s, saw something radically different. He believed objections were gifts, and the way you receive them determines whether a deal lives or dies. His "Submarine" technique, born from his obsession with psychological honesty in selling, transforms the entire power dynamic of a sales conversation.


Here's the insight that makes Sandler's approach different: when a prospect says "your price is too high" or "we're not ready to move forward," they're not blocking you. They're showing you exactly where the real negotiation needs to happen. The problem is that most salespeople respond with defense—better pricing, more features, longer timelines. Sandler realized that you have to go *deeper* first, like a submarine descending below the surface noise to find the actual concern underneath.


The technique works like this. When you encounter an objection, you don't counter it immediately. Instead, you acknowledge it with genuine curiosity, then ask a calibrated question that invites the prospect to explain more. You're essentially saying: "I hear your concern, and that tells me something important. Help me understand what's really going on here." This reframe is psychologically powerful because it stops the conversation from becoming adversarial. You're no longer arguing across a table; you're standing beside the prospect, looking at the problem together.


The magic happens in the next move. Rather than assuming you know what they mean, you get specific. If they say budget is tight, you might ask: "Is it that the funding isn't allocated yet, or is it that the total investment feels like too much value to prove in this first phase?" Notice the difference—you're not negotiating the price; you're diagnosing which fear is actually driving the objection. Often it's not the one you assumed.


Sandler's framework recognizes something neuroscientific: people don't make major buying decisions rationally, even in B2B. They make them emotionally and then rationalize them. An objection that sounds logical ("We need board approval") often masks an emotional one ("I'm not confident enough in this decision to champion it upstairs"). By going submarine—diving beneath surface objections—you access the real conversation. Once you understand the actual concern, you can address it directly rather than shadow-boxing with a proxy problem.


The power of this approach lies in its honesty. Prospects can feel when a salesperson is genuinely trying to understand versus when they're trying to overcome. Sandler teaches that if you can't resolve the real objection, you shouldn't force the deal anyway. That integrity builds long-term trust and referral relationships that generic closing techniques never achieve.


This framework has aged remarkably well, partly because it's rooted in human psychology rather than sales tactics. In 2026, when prospects are drowning in pitches and automation, the ability to ask a thoughtful question and actually listen for the real answer is more valuable than ever.


💡 One thing to try today: In your next sales call, when you hear an objection, pause for two full seconds before responding. Then ask one clarifying question that shows you're genuinely curious about what's underneath the stated concern—not trying to overcome it. Notice what you actually learn.
