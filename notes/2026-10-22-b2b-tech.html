Agentic Workflows: When Software Stops Waiting for Human Instructions


The most unsettling moment in a software demo happens when the AI stops asking permission. For years, we've built interfaces around confirmation dialogs—"Are you sure?"—as if the software were a cautious intern waiting for the boss's nod before sending an email. But something fundamental has shifted in the last eighteen months, and it's reshaping how enterprises actually work. Agentic workflows, where AI systems don't just suggest actions but *autonomously execute* multi-step business processes, have moved from research labs into production systems, and the implications are cascading through every layer of B2B software.


The distinction matters more than it sounds. A traditional SaaS tool—even one powered by modern language models—operates in a reactive mode. You input a query, it processes, it returns a result. You review it. You click "execute." You wait. Agentic systems flip this paradigm. They set a goal ("reconcile these invoices against our PO database"), break it into substeps, execute those steps across multiple systems, handle exceptions, and loop back to refine their approach when something unexpected emerges. Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's o1 model have made this possible not through raw speed but through reasoning depth—they can now navigate conditional logic and interdependencies that previously required explicit human programming.


The business case has proven itself in unglamorous but valuable places. Take expense management. A traditional solution requires employees to submit receipts, managers to review them, finance to audit them, and systems to record them. An agentic system reads the receipt, cross-references the employee's cost center, checks corporate policy rules in real time, verifies budget availability, auto-corrects common miscategorizations, flags genuinely suspicious entries for human review, and posts to the GL—all without a human touch except on exceptions. Companies like Ramp and Brex have quietly integrated these capabilities, and what's emerged is that time-to-expense-closure has dropped from ten days to six hours for 87% of submissions.


What makes this genuinely transformative—not just incrementally better—is that these workflows don't require perfect rule sets written in advance. Traditional automation demanded exquisite specification: "If Department A AND Amount > $5000 AND Category = Travel THEN escalate." The real world is messier. An agentic system learns from patterns, handles ambiguity, and adapts when rules conflict. It asks clarifying questions when genuinely uncertain rather than either blindly approving or stonewalling.


The catch, and this is what enterprises are grappling with now, is control. When software makes decisions autonomously, who's liable when it's wrong? Procurement teams are unusually nervous about this—the stakes are higher when purchasing decisions get made without explicit approval. This has forced vendors to build what might be called "transparent autonomy." Systems now generate comprehensive audit trails showing not just the decision but the reasoning chain: "I chose Vendor B because their pricing was 12% lower and lead time was 4 days shorter; I flagged the fact that they're new to our approved list for your team to review within 24 hours."


We're seeing a clustering around moderate autonomy rather than full independence. Most early adopters are comfortable with agentic workflows handling 60-70% of decisions (routine, low-risk, well-precedented) while maintaining human-in-the-loop for the remaining 30-40% (novel situations, high-value decisions, policy exceptions).


The interesting question isn't whether this becomes standard—it will. It's whether enterprises can rebuild their processes fast enough to capture the efficiency gains.


💡 One thing to try today: Map one routine business process your team does daily (like report generation or data entry validation) and ask: which steps could run autonomously if a system had confidence thresholds? Identify where you'd want human review gates.
