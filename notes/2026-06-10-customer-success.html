The Expansion Ratio: Why Your Best Revenue Hides in Plain Sight


There's a peculiar blindness that afflicts many customer success leaders. They obsess over churn, celebrate net retention above 100%, and pat themselves on the back when customers renew. But they miss the actual architecture of sustainable growth hiding inside their own accounts. That architecture is the expansion ratio—and it's far more important than most CS teams realize.


The expansion ratio measures the percentage of your customer base that increases their spend year-over-year. Unlike net retention, which can be artificially inflated by a few whales growing wildly while everyone else atrophies, the expansion ratio tells you something more dangerous and honest: how many of your customers are actually finding more value in your product over time? It's the customer health temperature taken across the entire patient population.


When Notion reported that their expansion ratio hovered around 130% net retention, it sounded impressive until you dug into the composition. The truth that emerged later: only about 40% of their customer base was expanding. The rest were stable or declining, and a small group of power users were carrying the entire metric. This is the expansion ratio's superpower—it forces you to confront the distribution of value creation rather than hide behind aggregate numbers.


Consider what a healthy expansion ratio actually means operationally. If 60% of your customers expand annually, it suggests that your CS team has cracked something fundamental: they understand which customers are positioned to grow, they've mapped the expansion motion (upsell, seat expansion, feature tier) that works for different segments, and they're executing it consistently. This is harder than it sounds. Most CS teams treat expansion like a side project, something the sales team "should handle," or worse, they treat all customers identically when expansion opportunities are wildly asymmetric.


The best CS organizations I've studied—companies like Slack, Datadog, and Figma in their growth phases—engineered their expansion ratios by segmenting customers into "expansion-ready" profiles. They asked: Which companies are hitting usage thresholds that indicate they've moved from pilot to critical? Which departments are adopting when we expected only one? Which industries are experiencing problems that our new features directly solve? They then assigned dedicated resources to those customers, treating expansion like a specialized motion rather than a tax on renewal management.


Here's what makes this insidious: a 50% expansion ratio can feel mediocre until you realize that if you're already at 85% net retention from core renewals, adding a 50% expansion ratio compounds into something close to 127% net revenue retention. That extra 40-50 basis points of growth isn't found in new logos or marketing efficiency—it's found in the customers you already have. It's the renewable revenue hiding in your installed base.


The metric also forces honesty about segmentation. If your expansion ratio is 35%, you have a problem. It means most customers aren't finding enough additional value to expand. That's not a CS execution problem—that's a product or positioning problem. Conversely, if it's 75%, you likely have a product that creates expanding value as customers scale, and your CS job becomes not "create expansion" but "accelerate customers toward the inevitable next purchase."


The expansion ratio is unglamorous. It doesn't make for good press releases. But it's the truest measure of whether your customers actually believe they need more of what you're selling.


💡 One thing to try today: Pull your customer database and calculate what percentage of your accounts increased their annual contract value (or committed spend) in the past 12 months. Compare that number to your net retention. If expansion ratio is more than 20 points lower than NRR, you've found your blind spot.
