Agentic Workflows: When Software Stops Waiting for Instructions


For decades, the promise of business software has been the same: automate repetitive tasks. But automation had a hard limit—it could only do what humans explicitly told it to do. You set up rules, configured workflows, and hoped the software would handle the rest. The moment something unexpected happened, everything stopped. Someone had to jump in, diagnose the problem, and write a new rule. It was automation with training wheels.


The shift happening right now is fundamentally different. Instead of building rigid workflows, enterprises are deploying what researchers call "agentic" systems—software that observes what's happening, reasons about next steps, and takes action autonomously within defined boundaries. Think of it less like a vending machine (press a button, get a specific output) and more like a capable colleague who understands context.


What makes this different from previous "intelligent automation" promises is the underlying technology. Large language models, combined with structured reasoning engines and real-time access to business data, can now handle ambiguity. A traditional workflow automation tool might get stuck if a customer's address format doesn't match expected patterns. An agentic system observes that the data is malformed, understands the business context (this is a customer account that matters), and intelligently resolves it—sometimes by asking for clarification, sometimes by making a reasonable inference, sometimes by flagging it for human review. Critically, it knows which path to take based on the specific situation.


Companies like Anthropic, OpenAI, and startups such as Zapier (with its Zapier Central), n8n, and Relevance AI have started releasing frameworks specifically designed for this. What's striking is that early adopters—particularly in finance, sales operations, and customer success—are reporting that agentic workflows reduce the need for manual intervention by 60-80% compared to traditional automation. Not because the automation is more rule-based, but because the system reasons its way through exceptions.


The business implication is profound. Right now, most enterprise software requires a whole layer of people—process managers, business analysts, RPA specialists—whose job is essentially translating business needs into logic the software understands. Agentic systems compress this. A product manager can describe what "successful customer onboarding" looks like, and the system figures out how to orchestrate it across CRM, email, billing, and support tools.


Of course, this introduces new risks. When software reasons autonomously, it can hallucinate, misinterpret, or take actions that seemed logical from incomplete data. The companies getting this right aren't replacing judgment—they're building transparency layers. Audit logs show exactly why the system made each decision. Humans remain in the loop for high-stakes decisions. The sweet spot is routine, well-understood business processes where context matters but the stakes are moderate.


We're still in the early innings. Most agentic tools today work best with structured business processes and clean data inputs. But the velocity of improvement is remarkable—what seemed sci-fi even eighteen months ago is now demo-able in a 30-minute implementation sprint. By next year, expecting software to reason rather than merely execute will be table stakes.


The real question isn't whether agentic workflows are coming. It's whether your organization is prepared to manage software that operates independently within its charter.


💡 One thing to try today: Take one recurring business process at your organization that currently requires manual exception-handling (like customer onboarding, expense approvals, or lead routing). Map out the three most common edge cases that break your existing automation. This is your test case for an agentic pilot.
